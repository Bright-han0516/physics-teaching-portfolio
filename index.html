01 / Interactive learning · Used in teaching

Making wave representations meaningful

The learning problem

Some students read a longitudinal-wave displacement graph as a picture of the medium. A correct-looking curve can conceal a misunderstanding of particle motion.

My contribution

I selected the concepts, checked the physics, and used AI-assisted coding to build browser simulations. The wave example places particle motion beside both graph types and allows a single particle to be followed frame by frame.

Evidence and limits

I designed 34 simulations across mechanics, waves and electricity. They have been shared with roughly 100 teachers; this is estimated sharing reach, not verified active use. Classroom walkthroughs suggested clearer understanding, but transfer to unfamiliar problems has not been reliably measured.

Next evaluation

Compare explanations before and after the activity, then use an unfamiliar wave question without the simulation. Score explanations with a clear rubric and examine persistent misconceptions. This is a proposed study, not a completed result.

02 / Formative feedback · Prototype paused

Whitespace: when should AI step back?

The learning problem

Fast answers can hide fragile understanding. I wanted a workflow that preserves the learner’s effort while making feedback manageable for a teacher.

My contribution

I designed an AI marking and hinting prototype using official marking criteria. Independent attempts and transfer checks are separated from supported practice. The interface presents scoring evidence and flags disagreements for teacher review. Implementation used AI-assisted coding.

What the initial test can tell us

I tested seven language models on 16 student responses. Reference scores were checked with AI assistance, without a dependable human benchmark. Agreement in that exercise cannot establish marking accuracy or learning benefit. Further development is paused until evaluation is reliable.

Next evaluation

Build a human-graded benchmark, resolve disagreements between raters, and evaluate feedback quality alongside scoring agreement. Test whether students can explain and solve a new problem without AI.

03 / Teacher development · Internal practice

AI tools with a teacher’s quality checks

The practical problem

AI can produce teaching materials quickly, but speed alone does not ensure coherent explanations, accurate physics or useful assessment tasks.

My contribution

Since January 2026, I have built 13 internal tools with AI assistance for curriculum work, producing notes across more than 50 units. I defined requirements, created a nine-dimension review checklist, and reviewed and corrected 11 batches of output.

Sharing the practice

I wrote a Tencent WorkBuddy guide adopted by centres in Shanghai, Beijing, Wuxi and Chongqing, trained around 60 teachers and 30 education advisers, and ran two workshops on AI in teaching. These describe delivery and reach, not measured learning gains.

What I want to investigate

Which review steps catch consequential errors, and which can be made less demanding? I want to connect teacher review effort with the quality of the learning tasks students actually receive.

03 / QUESTIONS TO PURSUE

From classroom impressions to dependable evidence

Reflecting on my teaching

An AI-assisted review of transcripts from 217 of my lessons prompted me to reconsider how quickly I answered my own questions. I now plan key questions and give students longer to explain. I treat that analysis as a prompt for reflection, not a precise measure of teaching quality.

A direction for graduate study

My next question is how teachers can review and calibrate AI-generated feedback efficiently without losing sight of student reasoning. I hope to study a human-in-the-loop formative assessment workflow for explanations in waves or electricity, using clear rubrics, human-graded responses and independent transfer tasks.
